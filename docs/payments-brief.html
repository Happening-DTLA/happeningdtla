Where The Money Lands

How a ticket payment travels from a buyer's card to your bank account, who carries the risk along the way, and the three decisions we need to settle before the October Art Night.

22 August 2026 Prepared for venue partners Decision needed by 29 August

The short version

If you read nothing else

We are recommending that each venue is the seller of record for its own tickets. Money from a ticket to your event is charged on your own Stripe account, your business name appears on the buyer's card statement, and DTLAHappening takes a clearly-stated fee off the top. We never hold your money.

The practical consequence is that you keep control and you get paid directly — and you also own the chargebacks and refunds for your own events, the same way you would if you sold the tickets yourself at the door.

To make that work, each venue needs to complete a Stripe onboarding form. That is the single item on the critical path, because verification takes days and cannot be rushed at the end. The ask today is that you start that form this week.

How the money actually moves

Plain language, no jargon

We use Stripe, the same payment processor behind most online checkouts. Stripe has a feature built for marketplaces — one platform, many independent businesses getting paid. That is exactly our shape: one app, many venues.

When someone buys a ticket, the money can be routed in one of three ways. They look identical to the buyer. They are very different for you. The difference comes down to one question: whose business is legally making the sale? That party is the merchant of record — the name on the statement, the one the bank comes to in a dispute, and the one who owes the refund.

Our recommendation: direct charges

Three reasons, in order of weight.

The liability belongs where the service is. If a show is cancelled or a buyer disputes a charge, the business that was going to provide the evening is the one holding the money and the one able to resolve it. Routing it any other way asks a two-person software company to guarantee your events, which is neither fair to us nor reassuring to you.

Your books stay clean. Your ticket revenue is reported on your own Stripe account and your own tax forms. Under the other models, every dollar of your ticket sales lands on our books first and then moves to you, which makes both sides' accounting messier than it needs to be.

You get paid on Stripe's normal schedule, from your own balance, without waiting on us to pass it along.

The trade-off we are accepting

Direct charges cannot combine several venues into one checkout. A buyer visiting three venues in one night pays three times rather than once.

For October this costs us nothing — every event is sold individually anyway. It matters later, when we build the "one wallet for the whole night" experience. We would rather solve that problem when we get to it than take on your chargeback risk today in exchange for a feature we have not built.

Stated plainly, so nobody is surprised

Under this model a chargeback on your event comes out of your balance. A buyer can dispute a card charge for months after the event, and banks frequently side with the cardholder. Two protections matter: keep your statement descriptor recognisable, and keep your own records of who actually walked in — the app's scan log gives you exactly that, timestamped.

One further caveat we want on the record rather than buried: if a venue's Stripe balance goes negative and stays there, Stripe can ultimately look to the platform to cover it. We are not asking you to indemnify us for that, but it is a real reason for us to care that every partner's account stays in good standing.

D2

Who absorbs the processing cost

Stripe charges roughly 2.9% + 30¢ on a standard US card. That cost exists no matter which platform you use. The only question is whether it comes out of your face value or is covered by the buyer's service fee.

Here is the same $15 ticket under the fee we currently have configured, and under a fee sized so that you receive the full face value.

The right-hand column is both better for you and cheaper for the buyer. That is not a trick — it is what happens when the fee is sized deliberately instead of picked as a round-sounding percentage. The current 6% + 99¢ was a placeholder in the code. We would like to replace it with a number chosen against this table.

Our recommendation is the second column: you always net the price you set. It makes your pricing decisions simple — set $15, receive $15 — and it means we only make money when the fee is genuinely covering our costs plus a stated margin.

Worth checking against real numbers

Please bring a recent Eventbrite payout statement to the next conversation. Comparing against what you actually netted per ticket last month is far more useful than comparing against their published rate card.

D3

Refunds and cancellations

This has to be written down before a single ticket is sold, because the policy has to be shown to the buyer at checkout. We need an answer to four questions per venue, or one shared answer across all venues.

  • Are tickets refundable at all, and if so, up to how many hours before doors?
  • Is the service fee refunded along with the face value, or kept?
  • If a venue cancels, is the refund automatic and immediate to every buyer?
  • If the whole night is called off — weather, power, city order — who announces it, and through what channel?

Our recommendation, as a starting point for discussion: full refunds including the service fee up to 24 hours before doors; automatic full refunds if the venue cancels at any point; and we absorb our own fee in a cancellation rather than profiting from an event that did not happen.

Why we want to be generous here

The whole proposition to a buyer is that this app is the only place to get these tickets. That only works if buying here feels at least as safe as buying anywhere else. A visible, generous refund policy is cheap insurance against the first bad night becoming the story people tell about us.

What we need from each venue

The critical path — please start this week

Stripe is legally required to verify every business that receives money. We cannot shortcut it, and neither can they. You will receive a link to a Stripe-hosted form — the information goes to Stripe, not to us.

  • Legal business name and structure (LLC, corporation, sole proprietor)
  • EIN, or Social Security number if you operate as a sole proprietor
  • Business address and phone number
  • A short description of what is being sold — "admission to live events"
  • One representative: legal name, date of birth, home address, and the last four of their SSN
  • Anyone owning 25% or more of the business, if it is a company
  • A bank account for payouts — routing and account number
  • A rough expected monthly volume and average ticket price

Most accounts verify within minutes. Some are asked for a photo of a driver's licence or a business document, which adds a day or two. A small number get stuck and need a real conversation with Stripe support. That last category is the reason we are asking now rather than in late September.

When you actually get paid

Stripe's normal US schedule pays out on a rolling basis a couple of business days after each charge. New accounts usually wait seven to fourteen days for the first payout while the account settles.

The thing most people are not told

Event ticketing is treated as higher risk than ordinary retail, because the buyer pays now for something delivered weeks later. If the event never happens, every one of those charges can come back as a dispute.

Because of that, Stripe may hold a reserve on a ticketing account, or delay payouts until after the event date. This is normal and not a mark against you — but it means you should not plan on ticket revenue landing in your account the morning after Art Night. We will describe the business accurately to Stripe during onboarding, since being upfront about selling event tickets is what avoids a payout freeze at the worst possible moment.

Still open — needs a lawyer, not us

We are software people. These are the items we have deliberately not answered, flagged so they do not get lost:

  • All-in pricing. California law now requires mandatory fees to be included in the advertised price rather than added at the end. The app already shows the full price everywhere, including on the browse screen — but somebody should confirm the current requirements apply to us the way we think they do.
  • Tax treatment of admissions and of the service fee itself, for both the venues and the platform. A CPA question, worth asking once, early.
  • Local licensing. Whether selling admissions through a platform changes anything about existing City of Los Angeles business registrations.
  • The exclusivity arrangement. "Tickets only on the app" should be in writing, and should say what happens if the app has an outage on a Thursday night. We would rather have that written down while everyone is friendly.
